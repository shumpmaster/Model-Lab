# Ruling needed: how we will know the screening worked, when we stop, and what happens to a method a small trial cannot prove

Milestone: Discovery-2

WHY: Every project must say from the start what it must improve, by how much, by when, and when it stops (the "Success defined" milestone). Setting this is yours alone: it is never delegated and never defaults. Until it is set, the plan cannot say how a method found by the screening is tried and kept, and the brief cannot go to you for the Design gate. If you do not decide, the screening waits here. One part of this card (part 3) asks whether a method may be tried on lighter evidence than the operating model asks for facts; that part departs from a written rule if you read the rule as covering methods to be tried, and it says so.

What you told us you want, in your words: "how multi agent systems are run and optimized. For example, when anthropic says “90% of their work is performed by Claude” I want to achieve that level of agentic efficiency, trust, success in the strategy ideation and coding work from my operating model", and "Faith to me meant that we test/adopt methods that are promising rather than only those that are fully credentialed" (both from your ruling on what the screening aims at).

**What our own records show today.** These come from a count of the three repositories' records up to 9 November: 447 merged pull requests (Q-017).
- **On the measure most often quoted outside, agents already do almost all the work here.** 94 to 99 commits in every 100 are made under an agent's name. A name is not proof of who did the work, but on that measure the 90% level is already passed, so it could not show any gain.
- **What the count cannot see is how often you had to step in.** About 15 in 100 of the desk's pull requests hold a change under your name, 7 in 100 of this repository's and 32 in 100 of the operating model's. Your account merged every pull request except 86 merged by a robot on the desk. Questions asked and answered in conversation leave no file, and your time is not recorded anywhere.
- **Whether work holds up is barely recorded.** No change was undone outright. Within 14 days, a later pull request touched the same files for about 2 in 3 of the desk's pull requests, but that is mostly ordinary follow-on work, not repairs; the records cannot tell the two apart.
- **From outside:** figures for "how much work AI does" count different things (characters, lines, functions) and come mostly from vendors with a stake. A rate of how often work is accepted means little unless it is paired with a check that the work was right (Q-016-P24, Q-016-P25).

**Words used below.** A **piece of work** is a merged pull request that changes built work, a plan or research; one that only files or answers a question, or only adds records, is not counted. A **touch** is a time you had to step in on a piece before it merged: a question put to you about it, a fix you made yourself, or a piece you sent back. Merging is not a touch, since you merge everything. A **trial** is a small test of one method on your own work against leaving things as they are. **Holds up** means two checks, both under every option: the share of pieces that pass their independent check at the first try is no lower, and the share that a later change reopens for a fault within 30 days is no higher. The second is marked in a new log by whoever makes the later change.

OPTIONS: in three parts, below, each ruled on its own and each ending with its own recommendation.

## Part 1: the measure and the target

  A. **Halve the share of work that needs you.** A new log counts touches, starting now; the share of pieces that needed at least one touch, over the last 30 pieces before the first trial, is the starting figure. *Target:* that share halved, with the work holding up. *Gain:* it measures what you said you want, "efficiency, trust", as less of you per piece, and it works from whatever the starting figure turns out to be. *Cost:* the log must be built before any trial, and questions asked in conversation must be logged by hand, so some may be missed; the starting figure is unknown until 30 pieces are logged. *Reversible:* yes.
  B. **Nine pieces in ten need no touch.** The same log and measure, with your own example level as a fixed target. *Gain:* it is the level you named. *Cost:* the 90% you quoted counts code written, not how often a person stepped in, so it is an example, not a like-for-like target; if the starting figure is already near nine in ten, the target says little, and if it is far below, it may be out of reach. Neither is known until the log has run. *Reversible:* yes.
  C. **Count from the records we already have.** The measure is the share of pieces that hold no change under your name, from the repositories' history alone; today about 85, 93 and 68 in 100 across the three. *Target:* nine in ten in each repository, with the work holding up. *Gain:* no new log, and a starting figure exists today. *Cost:* it misses questions and send-backs, which the records show are most of your involvement, and a name is not proof of who acted; this repository is already above nine in ten, so it could show no gain here. *Reversible:* yes.

RECOMMENDATION (part 1): A, with medium confidence. It is the only option that measures your involvement and works from any starting figure. What would change it: if you want your own 90% level as the target, B; if a new log is too much to add before the first trial, C.

## Part 2: how a trial decides, and what happens to one that ends "not shown"

**How a trial decides, under every option.** Each trial's size and rule are written down before it runs. A method is kept when its pieces need you less often than the pieces done just before it, by enough that chance alone would produce the difference at most 1 time in 10, and the work holds up. You said missing a helpful method and adopting a harmful one worry you "Both equally"; the prompt engine's line is stricter, 1 in 40, which misses more real gains. As an illustration, with 30 pieces on each side, a method that truly halves the share shows about 7 times in 10 if half the pieces need you today, and about 4 in 10 if 3 in 10 do (at 1 in 40: about 4 and 2 in 10). A method that does nothing is wrongly kept fewer than 1 time in 10. Most small trials of a real but modest gain will end **"not shown"**: the gain did not clear chance, which is not the same as "does not help" (Q-013).

  A. **One planned second look.** A "not shown" method that leans the right way and holds up stays on trial in this project, not in the operating model, for one more trial of the same size. Both trials are then judged together, by a rule fixed before the first began, so that the chance of wrongly keeping a method stays at 1 in 10 across both looks. Still not shown: it is dropped and its result is filed in the library as a result. A method that leans the wrong way, or does not hold up, is dropped at once. *Gain:* a promising method gets a fair second chance, which is where your "faith" applies, without lowering the bar. *Cost:* a second trial's pieces and your touches on them; methods wait longer for a verdict. *Reversible:* yes.
  B. **Drop at once.** A "not shown" method is dropped and its result filed; it may come back only with a trial plan that is truly different, as the operating model's experiment rules ask. *Gain:* simplest; least of your time; nothing unproven stays. *Cost:* the risk the plan names in advance: promising methods dropped after one small trial. *Reversible:* yes.
  C. **Keep on promise, with a review date.** A "not shown" method that holds up is kept in this project's way of working, reviewed after its next 30 pieces, and removed then if it still shows nothing. It reaches the operating model only after a trial shows a gain. *Gain:* the most room for faith. *Cost:* methods with no shown gain can build up between reviews, the risk of rules growing past what agents follow; in effect the bar to stay becomes "no harm seen". *Reversible:* yes.

RECOMMENDATION (part 2): A, with medium confidence. It gives a promising method a second chance and keeps the chance of keeping a useless one where it was set. What would change it: if your time on trials matters most, B; if you would rather keep a promising method in use while it proves itself, C.

## Part 3: what a method needs before it may be tried

  A. **One opened source describing it.** A method may be tried once one source someone has opened describes it, its conditions have been read against ours (clear misfits are not tried), and any outside code, prompt or skill has been checked before it runs, with no data out, as you ruled. Keeping it rests only on our own trial. The operating model asks two independent sources for any fact the work relies on; here no outside claim is relied on to keep a method, so the Chief of Staff reads the rule as not covering methods to be tried. That is a reading, not a ruling: choosing A rules it for this project, and it goes to the operating model as a proposal at the end of the cycle. *Gain:* new methods can be tried while they are new, as you asked. *Cost:* more trials of methods that turn out to be hype, each costing pieces and touches. *Reversible:* yes.
  B. **Two independent sources, as for any fact relied on.** *Gain:* fewer trials wasted on hype; no question about the rule. *Cost:* new methods often have one source, so they wait until a second appears, which works against your "promising rather than only those that are fully credentialed". *Reversible:* yes.

RECOMMENDATION (part 3): A, with medium confidence. It is the evidence standard you described, and our own trial still decides what is kept. What would change it: if you would rather spend fewer trials and accept adopting later, B.

RECOMMENDATION: A in all three parts; each part's reason is given after it.

**Proposed under every option, for the date, the miss and the stop rule** (proposals; your ruling makes them so):
  - **Date:** each trial is judged when its written size is done. The target in part 1 is checked six months after the first trial starts, and again at each screening review after that. To set no date, or another one, say so.
  - **Miss:** if the target is not reached at the check, you rule whether to re-aim the screening or close it. Methods already kept stay only on their own trial results; none is adjusted to pass ("killed, not tuned").
  - **Stop at once:** a trial stops if its work does not hold up beyond a line written before it starts, or if it breaks work you rely on. A change of AI model during a trial voids that trial.
  - **Stop and come back to you:** if the starting count shows fewer than 1 piece in 10 needs you (little room to gain); or if the first five trials all end with the method dropped (you rule whether to re-aim, for example with larger trials or other sources, or close).
  - **One at a time:** one method per kind of work at a time; no method that changes agents' instructions is tried while the prompt engine is testing that kind of work.
  - **Your time:** touches are the measure itself, and each status update reports them; your hours are not recorded anywhere. The ceilings on spend and on your time are set at the Design gate, as you ruled, and no trial runs before then.
