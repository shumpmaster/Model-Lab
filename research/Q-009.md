# Research question — Q-009

## The question

Groundwork map: how outside practice in building software and products with AI models and agents is found, judged and adopted, or not adopted, by an organisation that runs on a written operating method.

The organisation is a pipeline of AI agents under one human owner, described in shumpmaster/Personal-Org-Operating-Model (OPERATING_MODEL.md, and the role definitions in template-v3/.claude/agents/, template-v3/governance/BRIEFS.toml and PACKS.toml). Work moves through fixed phases, each agent session starts fresh from a written role and task brief, independent checkers review the work, and the method itself changes only through its own reviewed process. One product built under it is in use: a status page for the owner that shows each project's phase, milestones and open questions (shumpmaster/Service-Desk). A separate effort is already measuring whether changing the written instructions the agents run on affects how often their work is sent back. Outside practice here means the methods, tools, workflows and habits that practitioners, vendors and researchers report for building with AI models and agents, from formal engineering processes to informal styles of building mainly by prompting a model. "Screening" means any deliberate way of finding and judging such practice; whether to have one at all is part of the question.

Map the ground an expert would cover before deciding whether, and if so how, the organisation should look for and adopt outside practice. Where breadth and depth conflict, cover every area, even thinly:
- the baseline: what the method gains or loses with no deliberate screening, relying instead on ordinary model and vendor updates, its own review of its rules, or the owner's own reading; and what is lost by missing a real improvement, as well as by adopting a bad one;
- what it means for software or a product built with AI help to be ready for real business use, the standard any practice would be judged against, how that is judged or measured, by whom, and with what evidence;
- which kinds of source report outside practice (vendor documentation, practitioners' accounts, academic and industry studies, open-source projects, benchmarks), how reliable each kind is, how its claims are usually tested, and how much of it is itself machine-written, duplicated or promotional;
- how a claimed practice can be tested before it is adopted: on evidence alone, by a trial on the organisation's own work, or by a controlled comparison; and what each costs and can show when runs are few;
- how fast practice in this area changes, and what that means for whether any screening is one-off, repeated or not done, and for how long a finding stays valid;
- how a practice found elsewhere is checked for the conditions it depends on (team size, model, kind of product, human oversight) before it is carried into a different setting;
- how a written method takes in an outside change without growing past what its agents reliably follow; how the change is recorded, judged later to have helped, done nothing or harmed, and rolled back;
- how any screening would relate to the separate instruction-measuring effort, and how two efforts avoid measuring the same thing;
- whether, when and how an adopted change should reach a product already in use, such as the status page, or apply only to new work, and what each costs and risks;
- who would do the finding and judging (agents, the owner, or both), who checks them, and what it costs in agent runs, money and the owner's time; which decisions can only be the owner's;
- the risks of adopting outside tools, prompts or code: security, licences, data leaving the organisation, supply-chain compromise, and instructions planted in material being read;
- the risks on both sides: adopting what is popular rather than what works, sources with a stake in the answer, changes made faster than they can be checked, practices that pass checks but fail in use; and falling behind, or missing a change that would have helped;
- what other fields have learned about scanning outside practice and adopting it or declining it (for example technology radars, evidence reviews in medicine, standards bodies, and engineering change control), including where it was found to help little or to harm.

## The decision it serves

The shape of the first phase of this project, Discovery: which questions go to the owner, which go to research, and which need an experiment, before its value target, scope and plan are set.

## What each answer would change

Each area found to be unknown becomes a research request or a registered experiment; each found to be the owner's call goes on the owner's shortlist; each design trap found shapes the scope.
